the basics

ordered by distance from what you just watched on the other page.

the equations this page solves

reduced magnetohydrodynamics (RMHD), the equation set solved in the demos, is a simplified description of a plasma in a system with a strong background magnetic field (there are many restrictions on when it is appropriate, see if you can figure out when it would break down). it was originally derived in the context of the fusion program, independently by US and Soviet researchers. it turns out to also be really useful in the context of astrophysical turbulence!

  • Strauss 1976 — Nonlinear, three-dimensional magnetohydrodynamics of noncircular tokamaks. "original" (in the west!) derivation of RMHD, for fusion plasmas.
  • Kadomtsev & Pogutse 1974 — Nonlinear helical perturbations of a plasma in the tokamak. slightly earlier independent derivation, from the other side of the Iron Curtain.
  • Schekochihin et al. 2009 — Astrophysical gyrokinetics: kinetic and fluid turbulent cascades in magnetized weakly collisional plasmas. the "tome". §2 is a modern derivation of RMHD and of why it is the right limit; the rest is the kinetic story below it. (arXiv)
learning plasma physics

I (Alfred) owe a great deal to Alex Schekochihin's teaching, but if you want a gentler introduction, use Fitzpatrick (free and online!) or (one of my favourite books) Choudhuri. Matt Kunz notes are also extremely useful and complementary to Alex's course. you probably need to know some classical mechanics and E&M.

  • Schekochihin, Lectures on Kinetic Theory and Magnetohydrodynamics of Plasmas Oxford graduate notes: derives magnetohydrodynamics from kinetics.
  • Kunz, Lecture Notes on Irreversible Processes in Plasmas Princeton graduate notes; collisional/transport side done properly.
  • Choudhuri, The Physics of Fluids and Plasmas: An Introduction for Astrophysicists (CUP 1998). extremely good intuitive explanations; assumes an astronomer rather than a physicist.
  • Fitzpatrick, Plasma Physics: An Introduction / UT Austin notes. good, free, terse, online & searchable.
learning turbulence

a warning: turbulence in neutral fluids ("hydro") is usually taught as a completely different subject to MHD turbulence, despite many similarities. the Schekochihin paper here is a complete, modern, guided tour to the MHD side, while the Frisch and Davidson books are for the hydro side, in different but complementary styles.

  • Schekochihin 2022 — MHD turbulence: a biased review. the story of MHD turbulence from start to 2022: great fun to read! (arXiv)
  • Frisch 1995 — Turbulence: The Legacy of A. N. Kolmogorov. excellent, but with a particular style.
  • Davidson, Turbulence: An Introduction for Scientists and Engineers (OUP). also excellent, with physical intuition before formalism.
  • Biskamp, Magnetohydrodynamic Turbulence (CUP 2003). leaving this here as a classic reference that is no longer really up-to-date with the field.
heliophysics

the Sun is our closest star, and now with NASA's Parker Solar Probe, we have actually visited its outer atmosphere, the solar corona. the solar system generally is a great laboratory for plasma turbulence: throughout the space age, humanity has explored the solar system with spacecraft, taking increasingly detailed measurements of the plasma. here are some reviews.

  • Ervin et al. — The Sun as a driver of the inner heliosphere: modern investigations of fundamental plasma processes. an excellent and brief overview of what's important.
  • Bruno & Carbone 2013 — The Solar Wind as a Turbulence Laboratory. a classic Living Review of what has actually been measured.
  • Chen 2016 — Recent progress in astrophysical plasma turbulence from solar wind observations. shorter, and more focused on the turbulence theory questions. (arXiv)
  • Schrijver et al., Principles of Heliophysics free textbook, from the NASA summer schools.
  • HelioSwarm an upcoming mission, with a swarm of 9 separate spacecraft. designed specifically to take multi-point, state-of-the-art measurements of plasma turbulence.

deep lore (subjective)

a list of more specialized topics that I (Alfred) find particularly interesting, or have been personally involved in. these references are woefully incomplete, but hopefully at least give a flavour of the topics involved.

a brief history of (MHD) turbulence

from the mathematical foundation by Kolmogorov up to critical balance in MHD by Goldreich & Sridhar.

  • Kolmogorov 1941 — The local structure of turbulence in incompressible viscous fluid for very large Reynolds numbers and Dissipation of energy in the locally isotropic turbulence. three short papers that encode the great intuitive leap that spawned the whole turbulence field (the third, on the decay of isotropic turbulence, does not seem to be online). linked here in the 1991 Proceedings of the Royal Society translations.
  • Alfvén 1942 — Existence of electromagnetic–hydrodynamic waves. a two-page article in Nature, deriving the existence of the Alfvén wave: won the Nobel Prize in 1970.
  • Elsasser 1950 — The hydromagnetic equations. first noticed the usefulness of the z± = u ± b (“Elsasser”) variables for MHD.
  • Kraichnan 1965 — Inertial-range spectrum of hydromagnetic turbulence. used Elsasser's formulation of MHD for turbulence; predicted a −3/2 spectral index for the wrong reason (with Iroshnikov 1963, the Soviet equivalent).
  • Parker 1958 — Dynamics of the interplanetary gas and magnetic fields. the solar wind predicted before it was seen: one of the most amazing theoretical predictions in history. originally rejected by the reviewers, who were (fortunately) overruled by the editor (Chandrasekhar).
  • Coleman 1968 — Turbulence, viscosity, and dissipation in the solar-wind plasma. first observations that the solar wind is hotter than it should be, and how this can be explained via turbulence.
  • Montgomery & Turner 1981 and Shebalin, Matthaeus & Montgomery 1983. anisotropy arrives: the cascade sharpens structures across, but not along, the field.
  • Goldreich & Sridhar 1995 — Toward a theory of interstellar turbulence. II. Strong Alfvénic turbulence. critical balance, and a prediction for the scale-dependent alignment. start of the modern era of MHD turbulence theory.
dynamic alignment

numerical simulations of 3D MHD turbulence often stubbornly refuse to measure −5/3 spectra; instead typically obtaining −3/2. Boldyrev explained this with a very elegant argument: locally, velocity and magnetic field vectors dynamically align, causing the nonlinearity to deplete in a scale-dependent way. this was rather controversial, with others (e.g. Andrey Beresnyak) objecting, and claiming that alignment saturates and the asymptotic slope is −5/3. this was followed by nearly a decade of each group answering the other with ever-bigger simulations (an arms race up to resolutions of 4096 cubed!), and increasingly snarky replies. note that this resolution corresponds to inertial ranges still grossly insufficient for real astrophysical purposes. I (Alfred) was put to work on dynamic alignment as a short project at the start of my career; it ended up taking 7 years for us to figure out a way to resolve this.

  • Boldyrev 2006 — Spectrum of magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. alignment weakens the nonlinearity; the spectral index goes to −3/2.
  • Mason, Cattaneo & Boldyrev 2006 — Dynamic alignment in driven magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. numerical evidence for dynamic alignment.
  • Beresnyak 2011 — Spectral slope and Kolmogorov constant of MHD turbulence. the objection: alignment saturates, and the dissipative scale behaves as though the spectral index were −5/3 (read the paper to understand this argument in more detail)
  • Perez, Mason, Boldyrev & Cattaneo 2012 — On the energy spectrum of strong magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. the reply, at increased resolution.
  • Beresnyak 2013, Comment on Perez et al. and Perez et al. 2014, Comment on Beresnyak the argument in its purest form. read both.
reconnection takes over: the disruption scale

dynamically aligned eddies get more sheet-like as they cascade, which means that at some critical scale, they are thin enough for the tearing mode to break them up (see the tearing mode demo!). in other words, given enough resolution, the aligning cascade disrupts, forcing a transition to a new kind of turbulence: reconnecting turbulence, with a predicted spectral index of −11/5. this was predicted in 2017, simultaneously by two groups: Mallet, Schekochihin & Chandran, and Loureiro & Boldyrev: we discovered close to publication that we were working along the same lines, and agreed to put our preprints on arXiv on the same day. at the time I thought this theory was completely untestable numerically, but soon enough I was proved wrong by 10000²×5000 simulations by Dong et al. 2022, who largely confirmed our theory!

  • Mallet, Schekochihin & Chandran 2017 — Disruption of sheet-like structures in Alfvénic turbulence by magnetic reconnection.
  • Loureiro & Boldyrev 2017 — Role of magnetic reconnection in magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. the simultaneous one.
  • Boldyrev & Loureiro 2017 — Magnetohydrodynamic turbulence mediated by reconnection. what the spectrum below the disruption scale should look like; note that my original paper hedged between the −11/5 clearly predicted in this paper and −5/3, while Loureiro's original paper made a different prediction that turned out to be incorrect. both papers agreed on the position of the disruption scale.
  • Dong et al. 2022 — Reconnection-driven energy cascade in magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. the heroic simulation effort that confirms the pen-and-paper theory above.
  • Mallet, Schekochihin & Chandran 2017 — Disruption of Alfvénic turbulence by magnetic reconnection in a collisionless plasma. the same argument where the tearing is collisionless, which is the solar-wind-relevant case.
  • Vech et al. 2018 — Magnetic reconnection may control the ion-scale spectral break of solar wind turbulence. observational evidence that this is actually going on in the solar wind.
imbalance: an unsolved problem

turbulence in the solar wind and corona is strongly "imbalanced": z+>>z-, with Alfvén waves preferentially travelling away from the Sun. this allows an extra degree of freedom that has never been satisfactorily closed off: a dirty secret when it comes to applying the theory to the real world! these are some references, from the usual suspects. you can check out what happens in this case (at modest resolution) in the 3D forced turbulence demo by modifying epsilon+ and epsilon-.

  • Lithwick, Goldreich & Sridhar 2007 — Imbalanced strong MHD turbulence.
  • Beresnyak & Lazarian 2009 — Structure of stationary strong imbalanced turbulence.
  • Perez & Boldyrev 2010 — Numerical simulations of imbalanced strong magnetohydrodynamic turbulence.
  • Schekochihin 2022, § on imbalance all these models critically discussed; none of them wins.
reflection-driven turbulence

what sets the imbalance? waves launched from the Sun partially reflect from the gradients in density and magnetic field, driving the turbulent cascade. this means the turbulence is no longer homogeneous! taking this into account will be a major goal of turbulence theory for the next few years.

  • Dmitruk et al. 2002 — Coronal heating distribution due to low-frequency, wave-driven turbulence.
  • Chandran & Hollweg 2009 — Alfvén wave reflection and turbulent heating in the solar wind from 1 solar radius to 1 AU: an analytical treatment.
  • Chandran & Perez 2019 — Reflection-driven magnetohydrodynamic turbulence in the solar atmosphere and solar wind.
  • Meyrand et al. 2025 — Reflection-driven turbulence in the super-Alfvénic solar wind.
  • Chandran et al. 2025 — Intermittent, reflection-driven, strong imbalanced MHD turbulence. a recent theory combining intermittency (see below), reflections, critical balance, and imbalance; this seems to agree quite well with recent measurements.
how to measure anisotropy

it turns out that actually measuring critical balance is not trivial: because of the strong wandering of the field lines, the relevant mean field is the local one, including the fluctuations, so a standard Fourier transform would hide it. this was solved in simulations by Cho & Vishniac and Maron & Goldreich by using structure functions, and in the solar wind data by using wavelet transforms by Horbury et al. in simulations, we can also carefully step along the field lines and take Fourier transforms, which is what is done in the demos.

  • Cho & Vishniac 2000 — The anisotropy of magnetohydrodynamic Alfvénic turbulence. anisotropy measured in the local field in simulations, for the first time.
  • Maron & Goldreich 2001 — Simulations of incompressible magnetohydrodynamic turbulence.
  • Horbury, Forman & Oughton 2008 — Anisotropic scaling of magnetohydrodynamic turbulence. the same idea applied to spacecraft data with wavelets.
  • Mallet et al. 2016 — Measures of three-dimensional anisotropy and intermittency in strong Alfvénic turbulence. anisotropic in three directions, not two: including the effects of dynamic alignment, the fluctuations are ribbons.
weak turbulence, and the transition to strong

if the nonlinear time is much longer than the Alfvén propagation time, one can do formal perturbation theory. this beautiful but slightly arcane theory was worked out around the turn of the millennium. worth noting that most observed Alfvénic turbulence is, in fact, strong, and even if weak at large scales, it becomes strong: see Meyrand et al. (numerics) and Zhao et al. (observations) below. what happens to the spectrum & anisotropy in the 3D forced demo if you make the energy injection rate as small as possible?

  • Galtier, Nazarenko, Newell & Pouquet 2000 — A weak turbulence theory for incompressible magnetohydrodynamics.
  • Meyrand, Galtier & Kiyani 2016 — Direct evidence of the transition from weak to strong MHD turbulence.
  • Zhao et al. 2024 — Identification of the weak-to-strong transition in Alfvénic turbulence from space plasma. the transition, measured rather than simulated: four Cluster spacecraft in Earth's magnetosheath.
intermittency

the spectrum isn't the whole story! most of the dissipation is concentrated in a small part of the volume, in large coherent structures. this breaks the self-similarity of Kolmogorov 1941: the fix is a more complex statistical model, which in MHD has to include the effects of critical balance and dynamic alignment.

  • Kolmogorov 1962 (K62) — the refined similarity hypothesis. Kolmogorov's own correction to Kolmogorov: apparently Landau quickly realised a hole in his 1941 argument, but it took a while to sort out.
  • She & Leveque 1994 — Universal scaling laws in fully developed turbulence. the log-Poisson picture that the MHD theories below inherit.
  • Salem et al. 2007 — Anomalous scaling and the role of intermittency in solar wind MHD turbulence: new insights.
  • Mallet et al. 2015 — Refined critical balance in strong Alfvénic turbulence.
  • Chandran, Schekochihin & Mallet 2015 — Intermittency and alignment in strong RMHD turbulence.
  • Mallet & Schekochihin 2016 — A statistical model of three-dimensional anisotropy and intermittency in strong Alfvénic turbulence. critical balance, alignment and intermittency in one model.
  • Mallet et al. 2019 — Interplay between intermittency and dissipation in collisionless plasma turbulence. why intermittency matters: it dramatically affects how heating happens in a real collisionless plasma.
collisionless turbulence and gyrokinetics

at small enough scales, the ion's gyration around the magnetic field has a larger radius than the size of the eddies, and RMHD breaks down. what happens then? a good framework for this is gyrokinetics: another model derived first in the fusion world and later applied very successfully to astrophysics. more recently, the discovery of the helicity barrier by Meyrand et al. has revolutionized this field: showing that small-scale effects can break even the most fundamental rules of turbulence.

  • Schekochihin et al. 2009 — Astrophysical gyrokinetics. the "tome": the whole framework of gyrokinetics applied to astrophysics. have fun!
  • Howes et al. 2008 — Kinetic simulations of magnetized turbulence in astrophysical plasmas. first gyrokinetic simulation of the sub-ion-gyroradius turbulence.
  • Meyrand, Squire, Schekochihin & Dorland 2021 — On the violation of the zeroth law of turbulence in space plasmas. the helicity barrier; probably the most important discovery for astrophysical plasma turbulence since (at least) Boldyrev 2006. (arXiv)
  • Squire et al. 2022 — High-frequency heating of the solar wind triggered by low-frequency turbulence.
  • Meyrand et al. 2023 — Mediation of collisionless turbulent dissipation through cyclotron resonance.
  • McIntyre et al. 2025 — Evidence for the helicity barrier from measurements of the turbulence transition range in the solar wind. the observational test.
collisionless high-beta dynamics: firehose and mirror

when the magnetic field is relatively weak (compared to the thermal pressure), the plasma pressure can be anisotropic: this drives a whole zoo of instabilities, altering the picture of Alfvénic turbulence based on the RMHD equations. interestingly, this is the case in many astrophysical environments, e.g. the intracluster medium, accretion disks, and (some of) the solar wind.

  • Bale et al. 2009 — Magnetic fluctuation power near proton temperature anisotropy instability thresholds in the solar wind. observational evidence of instabilities bounding the observed turbulence in the solar wind.
  • Squire, Quataert & Schekochihin 2016 — A stringent limit on the amplitude of Alfvénic perturbations in high-beta low-collisionality plasmas. the interruption.
  • Squire, Kunz, Quataert & Schekochihin 2017 — Kinetic simulations of the interruption of large-amplitude shear-Alfvén waves in a high-beta plasma.
  • Squire, Schekochihin, Quataert & Kunz 2019 — Magneto-immutable turbulence in weakly collisional plasmas. turbulent self-organisation to avoid the problem.
how plasma gets so hot

the solar coronal plasma is orders of magnitude hotter than the photosphere: we think this is because of turbulence (or reconnection, according to some). what is difficult is to explain why the ions are heated: because of the anisotropic cascade, the fluctuations were expected to be unable to access the cyclotron resonance: the helicity barrier (see above) may be a way out. recently, Parker Solar Probe observations have allowed us to make serious progress in how this heating occurs.

  • Kawazura, Barnes & Schekochihin 2019 — Thermal disequilibration of ions and electrons by collisionless plasma turbulence. heating within the gyrokinetic model: no ion heating at low beta like in the corona.
  • Chandran et al. 2010 — Perpendicular ion heating by low-frequency Alfvén-wave turbulence in the solar wind. stochastic heating: getting around the low frequency.
  • Bowen et al. 2024 — Extended cyclotron resonant heating of the turbulent solar wind. close to the Sun, we see a lot of quasi-parallel cyclotron waves. we think these are not driven directly by the turbulence, but by the strong stochastic heating resulting from the more perpendicular turbulent fluctuations (see below).
  • Mallet et al. 2026 — Perpendicular ion heating in turbulence and reconnection: magnetic moment breaking by coherent fluctuations. an updated and scale-dependent model for stochastic heating, derived from first principles.
  • Bowen et al. 2025 — Stochastic heating in the sub-Alfvénic solar wind, and Ervin et al. 2026 — Direct measurement of diffusion coefficients: evidence for diffusive stochastic heating in collisionless plasmas. observational evidence for ion heating via stochastic heating in the sub-Alfvénic solar wind.
  • Chael et al. 2018 — The role of electron heating physics in images and variability of the Galactic Centre black hole Sagittarius A*. numerical study of accretion-disk heating, showing the dramatic observational consequences of different heating models.
switchbacks

one of Parker Solar Probe's most interesting discoveries was that the magnetic field near the Sun is dominated by sharp, quasi-discontinuous rotations of the magnetic field, dubbed switchbacks since they can even reverse its direction completely. whether this should have been surprising is unclear (see Goldstein et al. 1974 below). nevertheless, this has spawned a frenzy of theoretical explanations. my own point of view: the switchbacks are simply large-amplitude Alfvén waves (Alfvén tsunamis?), that grow in normalized amplitude due to the gradients in the background as they propagate away from the Sun, in the same way that water waves steepen and break as they approach the beach.

  • Bale et al. 2019 — Highly structured slow solar wind emerging from an equatorial coronal hole, and Kasper et al. 2019 — Alfvénic velocity spikes and rotational flows in the near-Sun solar wind. the discovery papers.
  • Squire, Chandran & Meyrand 2020 — In-situ switchback formation in the expanding solar wind.
  • Mallet et al. 2021 — Evolution of large-amplitude Alfvén waves and generation of switchbacks in the expanding solar wind.
  • Goldstein et al. 1974 — On the theory of large amplitude Alfvén waves.
magnetic reconnection: another 50-year problem

without dissipation, in MHD the magnetic field lines are frozen into the flow. with resistivity (or kinetic effects!) added, they can "reconnect", driving heating and fast flows: the classic Sweet–Parker theory, however, predicts rates that are far too slow. this can be resolved either with kinetic effects (Drake et al.) or by taking the tearing instability of the sheet into account (Loureiro's plasmoid instability).

  • Sweet 1958 — The neutral point theory of solar flares, and Parker 1957 — Sweet's mechanism for merging magnetic fields in conducting fluids. the original picture, and the rate that turned out to be far too slow.
  • Loureiro, Schekochihin & Cowley 2007 — Instability of current sheets and formation of plasmoid chains. Sweet–Parker sheets are themselves unstable, which is why the whole picture changed.
  • Drake, Shay & Swisdak 2008 — The Hall fields and fast magnetic reconnection. the kinetic route to a fast rate.
  • Uzdensky & Loureiro 2016 — Magnetic reconnection onset via disruption of a forming current sheet by the tearing instability. the modern framework for the plasmoid/tearing instability and reconnection onset.
turbulent dynamo

everything else on this page assumes a strong background magnetic field. where did it come from? turbulence amplifies a seed field exponentially, which is a different problem with a different literature — and in a collisionless plasma it is a genuinely open one. outside my area of expertise, but here is a review and a couple of recent papers on the collisionless problem.

  • Rincon 2019 — Dynamo theories. the review to start from.
  • St-Onge, Kunz, Squire & Schekochihin 2020 — Fluctuation dynamo in a weakly collisional plasma.
  • Zhou et al. 2024 — Magnetogenesis in a collisionless plasma: from Weibel instability to turbulent dynamo.
turbulence, everywhere

a few more examples of turbulence throughout the universe.

  • Armstrong, Rickett & Spangler 1995 — Electron density power spectrum in the local interstellar medium. the “big power law in the sky”. a single power law spectrum over an absurd range of scales.
  • Balbus & Hawley 1991 — A powerful local shear instability in weakly magnetized disks. I. Linear analysis. why accretion disks are turbulent at all: the magnetorotational instability.
  • Zhuravleva et al. 2014 — Turbulent heating in galaxy clusters brightest in X-rays. turbulent heating in the largest gravitationally bound objects in the universe, galaxy clusters.
  • Galtier, Laurie & Nazarenko 2020 — A plausible model of inflation driven by strong gravitational wave turbulence. non-plasma: a model of inflation resulting from an inverse cascade of gravitational waves (!)
disclaimer: simulations lie

a caveat on all numerical simulations. we are limited in the length of the inertial range by the resolution available on our machines; even with massively parallelized codes, this is still far smaller than even the solar wind inertial range, let alone the interstellar medium! this means we cannot expect to rigorously get the intermittency and heating correct: we have to use our intuition and experience to argue for the broader applicability of our tools and theories. this causes serious arguments that last decades (see dynamic alignment, for example). moreover, we also use highly simplified models (like RMHD), with simplified driving, and simplified dissipation. this is a blessing and a curse: we can switch effects on and off with ease, but we have to be careful not to over-interpret our results: the test is always ultimately in nature.
