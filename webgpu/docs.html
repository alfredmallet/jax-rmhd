how to use these pages

every button and every chart in the 2D and 3D RMHD apps · 2D app · 3D app · further reading

Both apps have the same shape: a what is all this? intro under the title (open until you close it once), a 2D / 3D tab pair that switches between them, a bar at the top that is always visible, a panel of settings under it (hidden until you press show params), and below that a column of display cards (pictures of the field) beside a column of chart cards (graphs against time or against scale). Off to the side, or under everything on a narrow screen, is the background reading — turbulence, plasma and why any of it matters. Nothing is modal — you can change any setting at any time, and the run keeps going. The 3D app adds the things a third dimension needs; everything else is identical.

A good first move is to pick a preset from the bar at the top, press the green Run, and change one thing at a time.

the bar at the top

Run starts the simulation and turns into Pause. Pausing freezes time; the displays and charts stay where they are and every control still works, so you can set something up while paused and then run.

Reset rebuilds the initial condition and puts the clock back to zero, using whatever the initial-condition settings currently say. It does not touch your displays or charts.

preset is a drop-down of ready-made configurations. Picking one sets the controls and opens the displays and charts that go with it, then prints a one-paragraph note about what to look for. That note stays at the top of the screen with the bar while you scroll. Presets are configurations, not separate programs: everything stays adjustable afterwards. Each one also has a direct link — for example rmhd2d.html?demo=kh — which opens the page already set up that way.

show params / hide params opens and closes the whole settings panel: it starts closed, so the first thing on screen is the picture, and the page remembers whichever you chose. hide text does the same for the preset's note. Both hide, neither resets.

On the right of the bar is a readout of the simulation time t, the step number, the current time step dt, and how many steps per second your GPU is managing. Under the displays there is a second readout with the kinetic and magnetic energies and the two forcing scales.

simulation settings

resolution is the number of grid points across the box. Higher is sharper and slower. Changing it restarts the run.

box (2D only) is the shape of the box: square 2π×2π, wide 4π×2π, or large 8π×8π. The wide box exists for the shear-layer and tearing demos, which need room along x for one unstable wavelength along y; the large box is simply four times bigger, which is what lets one thin layer hold many unstable wavelengths along it so it can pick its own. Resolution means the number of points across x, and the other axis follows the box. (1024 points is the longest line the transforms will build, so a resolution a given box cannot reach is greyed out in the list rather than quietly reduced.)

Lz (3D only) is the length of the box along the strong background magnetic field, which points along z. Longer boxes hold longer wave packets.

seed is the random seed. The same seed gives the same run.

cfl_safety and cfl_every control the time step. The solver picks dt from how fast the field is moving; cfl_safety is the fraction of the largest stable step it takes (smaller = safer and slower), and cfl_every is how many steps it waits before recomputing it. The defaults are fine unless a run blows up.

dissipation

Dissipation is what removes energy at small scales, so the cascade has somewhere to end. Too little and the smallest scales pile up into noise; too much and there is no turbulence, just a slow smooth decay.

hyper chooses how sharply dissipation switches on with scale. 1 is ordinary viscosity and resistivity. Higher values leave the large scales almost untouched and then bite hard at the grid scale, which buys a wider clean range of scales — useful for looking at spectra, less honest if you care about the physics of the dissipation itself.

diss is the strength, on a logarithmic slider. Its range is recomputed for the current resolution and hyper value, so one sweep of the slider always goes from "too thick to be turbulent" at the top to "too thin for this grid" at the bottom.

auto (ticked by default) sets diss for you, continuously, from the amount of energy actually measured near the smallest resolved scale. While it is ticked the slider is disabled but still shows the value it has chosen, so you can watch it move; untick it and the slider stays exactly where the controller left it. The tearing and rmhd-variables presets turn it off: tearing quotes a growth rate against a fixed resistivity.

Pm (2D only) is the magnetic Prandtl number. The diss slider is the resistivity η (acting on ψ), and the viscosity acting on φ is Pm×η. 1 means both are equal. 0 means no viscosity at all.

z_diss_k (3D only) damps structure along the background field. It has its own auto button, which sets it from the grid.

forcing

Forcing is how energy gets put in. With the checkbox off the run is decaying turbulence: it starts with whatever the initial condition gave it and runs down.

ε+ and ε− are the rates at which energy is injected into the two Elsasser fields, on logarithmic sliders. They are in the same units and the total injection rate is their sum. The lock checkbox moves them together; unlock them to drive an imbalanced cascade, where more energy travels one way along the field than the other.

tau is the correlation time of the random forcing: how long a given random kick stays pointing the same way before it is refreshed.

band n is a pair of handles setting which scales are forced, in units of the box wavenumber — the default 1–3 forces the largest few scales and lets everything smaller be produced by the cascade. They cannot cross. Moving them restarts the solver when you let go.

initial condition

The preset drop-down inside this group chooses what the run starts from. Reset re-applies it.

  • large-scale modes — a fixed pattern of a few big waves. The default, and the state the self-test is defined on.
  • letters A / B — an A-shaped and a B-shaped blob, one in each Elsasser field, smoothed to a physical width. In 3D they are wave packets: the same shapes wrapped in a bell-shaped envelope along z, placed to collide head-on.
  • custom (drawn blobs) — whatever you paint. See the next section.
  • quiescent (zero) — exactly nothing. Useful with forcing on, to watch turbulence build from a standing start.
  • Kelvin–Helmholtz shear layers and tearing mode (2D only) — two equilibria plus a small seed perturbation, which then grow. They bring their own physical knobs (below).
  • sinusoidal z± packets (3D only) — the textbook wave collision, and what the collision demo opens on: one packet is a single wave in x, the other a single wave in y, so their interaction is one clean beat instead of a spectrum of them, and each fills the box rather than sitting in it as a blob.

The two amp sliders set how strong the starting fields are. Normally they are labelled ζ+ amp and ζ− amp and set the peak strength of each Elsasser field separately; lock moves them together. They rescale the stored initial condition, so you can pause, move a slider and press Reset to get the same shape at a different strength, any number of times. They appear only for the presets they apply to — the letter and sinusoid packets and the drawing. large-scale modes and quiescent have no amplitude to set, and the equilibrium demos below set theirs with their own physical knobs instead, so in those four the amp rows are simply not shown.

While you are painting a custom drawing in φ or ψ, the same two sliders are relabelled φ amp and ψ amp and mean something more direct: φ amp is the peak flow speed max |u|, and ψ amp is the peak perpendicular field strength max |b|. That is the only way to ask for something like "a strong vortex plus a weak magnetic island", which is not expressible as a pair of Elsasser amplitudes. Painting ζ+/ζ− puts the old labels back.

σz (3D only) is the length of the wave packets along the background field. It also sets how nonlinear the collision is: the line under these controls prints χ± (well below 1 = a weak collision, the packets pass through each other nearly unchanged; around 1 or more = a strong one, a single collision already shreds them) together with the time at which the two packets meet. There is a cap on σz; increase Lz if you want longer packets.

The equilibrium demos add their own row: a / Lx is the width of the layer, seed the size of the perturbation that grows out of it, U0 the shear-flow strength and b0 the in-plane magnetic field for Kelvin–Helmholtz, and ψ0 the current-sheet strength for tearing. maintain equilibrium flux (tearing) holds the equilibrium against its own slow decay, so what you measure is the instability and not the layer spreading out; switch it off and the measured growth rate drops noticeably. broadband seed (tearing) replaces the single seeded wavelength with every wavelength the layer could go unstable to, at equal size and random phase, so the layer selects its own instead of being handed one — which is what the island chain demo is built on. The island-width chart switches off while it is ticked: that chart measures one island, and this seeds many.

drawing your own initial condition

Choose IC custom (drawn blobs) and press edit IC. The run pauses and the displays are replaced by a single drawing canvas. Click or drag: each stroke deposits a smooth round blob.

paint chooses which field the stroke goes into: φ (a vortex), ψ (a magnetic island), or the two Elsasser potentials ζ+ and ζ− (a wave travelling one way or the other along the field). σ⊥ is the blob width, as a fraction of the box. negative flips the sign of a stroke — so does dragging with the right mouse button. In 3D the editor has its own z-plane slider, and each blob is also given a length along z.

Leaving the editor: save & run keeps the drawing, applies it and resumes; save keeps it and stays paused; cancel throws away everything drawn since you opened the editor. clear empties the canvas without leaving. There is no undo.

The strength of what you drew is set by the amp sliders afterwards, not by how hard you scribbled: the whole drawing is rescaled to the amplitude you ask for. The drawing is dropped if you change the resolution, because it is stored on the grid.

display cards — the pictures

+ display in the "displays & charts" group adds a card (up to three); the × on a card's header closes it. Every card is independent, so you can watch three different quantities of the same run side by side.

The first drop-down is what is drawn. The caption under the picture repeats it with its definition:

  • velocity |u|: how fast the plasma is moving, u = ẑ×∇φ.
  • magnetic |b|: the strength of the field in the plane, b = ẑ×∇ψ.
  • φ and ψ: the two potentials the solver actually evolves. Contours of φ are the streamlines of the flow; contours of ψ are the perpendicular magnetic field lines.
  • vorticity: ω = ∇²φ, the local rate of spin of the flow.
  • current: j = ∇²ψ, the electric current. Thin bright sheets of it are where reconnection happens.
  • Elsasser ω+ and ω−: ω± = ∇²ζ± = ω ± j, where ζ± = φ ± ψ are the potentials of z±. Each is the vorticity of one travelling wave on its own: a packet moving one way along the field shows up in one of them and is invisible in the other, which is how you tell two colliding packets apart.
  • Elsasser |z+| and |z−|: z± = u ± b, the two waves travelling in opposite directions along the background field. Turbulence needs both: a lone z+ travels unchanged.
  • cross helicity σc: 2u·b/(|u|²+|b|²), how aligned the flow and the field are, between −1 and +1. Turbulence tends to align them, which weakens the interaction.
  • residual energy σr: (|u|²−|b|²)/(|u|²+|b|²), which of the two dominates locally: +1 all flow, −1 all field, 0 equal.

arrows overlays the direction of the field on the magnitude modes (|u|, |b|, |z±|).

contours draws in-plane field lines over the picture: ψ contours are magnetic field lines, φ contours are streamlines, and ψ + φ draws both at once in two colours, which is how you see whether flow and field are aligned. Two more drop-downs appear with them: the number of contour levels, and whether to draw them over the colour field or over a plain bg — a blank plate, for when the colours underneath are in the way. The lines are evenly spaced in value, so they crowd together where the field is strong.

The last drop-down is the colormap: afmhot, viridis, RdBu or grayscale, per card.

A narrow filter kmin slider appears on every card once you tick k⊥ filter in the displays & charts panel: it is the scale filter, and it drops everything larger than the size you set. It is off to begin with, and it changes the picture only — never the run.

In 3D each card also has a view drop-down, and it opens on volume — the whole box, drawn see-through, which has its own section below. z slice shows one plane, picked by the slider beside it — a slider that is only there in that mode, since the other views have no single plane to pick. track z+ and track z− follow a wave packet as it moves, so the display rides along with it. cube faces draws the three visible outer faces of the box instead of one plane (the top face is the card's own plane, so a tracking cube card puts the collision on top). field lines draws the magnetic field lines themselves through the whole box, with the box outline; the field selector and the arrows do nothing in that view, but the contour selector still inks the top face. Next to + display / + chart there is a page-wide tracking drop-down deciding how those trackers follow a packet: energy centroid (smooth, the default) or peak plane (the single strongest plane, which jumps about more) — and, beside it, true box shape, which is what draws the box with its real edge lengths. Untick it and the box is drawn as a cube whatever Lz says, which is how these pages looked before.

Along the bottom of each card, on the caption line: a colorbar showing the card's colormap and the range the colours currently mean, and the save and rec buttons.

the 3D volume view

volume is the view a 3D display card opens on, and the one the Alfvén-wave collision preset is built around. Instead of one plane, or the three outer faces, it draws the whole box see-through: for every pixel on screen it shoots a ray through the box and adds up what that ray passes through. The box is drawn with its real proportions, so a long Lz is a long column and not a cube — untick true box shape in the displays & charts panel if you would rather have the cube back.

What it draws is not the field everywhere — that would be fog — but two surfaces of constant value, one at +level and one at −level of the range the colorbar is showing, softened into shells rather than left as hard skins. At these resolutions a hard surface shows the grid it was built on and a soft one does not. The positive shell takes one end of the card's colormap and the negative shell the other, the same colours the slice views use, so the two agree. Quantities that can only be positive (|u|, |b|, |z±|) get the one shell instead of two.

The two labelled sliders in the card header belong to this view and appear only in it. % range is where the shells sit, as a fraction of the colour range: low picks up weak structure and fills the box, high keeps only the strongest cores. How thick the shells are follows that level (it is 0.4 of it), so there is no third slider. opacity is how solidly they draw — low is a haze you can see through, high and the front of the box hides the back of it. Both are per card, and both are only how the picture is drawn: the simulation neither sees them nor cares.

Two things change in the card header. The arrows checkbox becomes lines and overlays the magnetic field lines through the whole box; it is off to begin with, because the lines are drawn on top of the picture rather than woven into it, so a line that ought to be behind a shell will still be in front of it. And the contour controls disappear: contours are drawn on a plane, and there is no plane here.

The two σ quantities cannot be volume-rendered — they are built from four fields at once and a card has room for two — so a card asking for σc or σr in this view quietly shows cube faces instead, and its two sliders hide with the view they belong to.

Marching a volume is the most expensive drawing these pages do. If a card feels heavy, one volume card with the others on slices is a fair trade — and the picture does not change with the machine: the shells sit where the sliders say either way.

the scale filter — looking at the small scales on their own

Tick k⊥ filter in the displays & charts panel and every display card grows a narrow filter kmin slider: a filter on the picture, in both apps. It keeps only structures smaller than a size you choose — k⊥ at or above the handle, counted in box wavenumbers, which is the unit the spectrum chart's horizontal axis uses, so where you put the handle is a place on that axis. The top of the range is always the dealias cut, i.e. the smallest thing the run resolves. At 0 the filter is off, and off is exactly off: the picture is then the same picture, pixel for pixel, as if the slider did not exist. Untick the box and the sliders go away again.

It filters the picture and nothing else. The simulation is not filtered: the run, the time step, the energies, the spectra and the field lines all carry the whole field. (Saving or recording a filtered card saves the filtered picture — what you see is what you get.) Two cards can show two different cuts of the same instant side by side, which is what the control is for. The caption says filter: k⊥ = 10:kmax whenever it is active, because a filtered picture looks like a different simulation and is not one.

Why bother: turbulence is many sizes at once, and the big structures are the bright ones, so a raw snapshot mostly shows the big ones. Slide the handle up during a developed run and the picture rescales to what is left — sheets and eddies that get thinner across the magnetic field as you go up, while staying long along it. That growing elongation is the same thing the anisotropy k∥/k⊥ chart measures as a falling curve; here you are looking at it directly. In 2D the same cut shows the small-scale sheets and the alignment between flow and field. Filtered snapshots like these are how the anisotropy was first measured (Cho & Vishniac; Maron & Goldreich) — the difference is that here the scale is a slider and the run keeps going. The 2D spectrum chart does the same filtering as a measurement rather than as a picture.

Practical notes. The edge of the filter is soft, about one wavenumber wide, on purpose: a hard cut rings, and the ringing would look like structure that is not there. What is left high up is a small amount of energy, and the colour scale follows the filtered field, so it will amplify whatever is there — including grid noise, at the very top of the range. That is the honest reason the sliders are off until you ask for them: it is a tool for a developed run, not a knob for a first look. In 3D it applies to the volume view too, which is the payoff: a filtered j∥ in a long box, one range of scales at a time.

the colorbar

The strip on the right of the caption line is the card's own colormap, with three numbers under it: the value at the left end, in the middle, and at the right end.

Which range it shows follows the quantity:

  • signed quantities (φ, ψ, vorticity, current) are drawn symmetrically about zero, so the bar reads −max, 0, +max.
  • magnitudes (|u|, |b|, |z±|) can only be positive, so it reads 0 to max.
  • σc and σr are always drawn on a fixed −1…+1 scale, so their colours mean the same thing from frame to frame and between runs.

Everything else rescales itself continuously to whatever is in the picture, which is why the numbers move while the run does. That is worth remembering when comparing two moments: the colours are the same, the numbers are not. The bar is hidden in the 3D field-lines view, which draws no colour field.

chart cards — the graphs

+ chart adds one; the drop-down in its header chooses the type, and the × closes it. You can have several of the same type with different options.

energy trace — energies against time. The option chooses E_u, E_b (kinetic and magnetic) or E+, E− (the two Elsasser energies, which is what tells you whether the cascade is imbalanced). The total is drawn in both.

spectra — how energy is distributed across scales, on log axes, so a power law is a straight line. Options:

  • which spectra: E_u, E_b · E+, E− · both.
  • direction (3D): across the field (⊥), along it (∥, dashed), both, or ⊥ + k∥ (field line), which measures the parallel spectrum along the actual curved magnetic field rather than along the z axis.
  • fit: a straight reference line E = A kp. pin to field places it on the spectrum itself, set A lets you type the amplitude, and off hides it. The index box next to it defaults to −5/3; type −1.5 and it is labelled −3/2. Pinned, the line is matched to the spectrum at an intermediate scale — halfway, logarithmically, between the box scale and the dissipation scale — so it runs through the middle of the resolved range instead of being tied to the noisy end of it. Before there is a dissipation range to speak of (the first frames, or a run with no knee in view) it falls back to matching just above the forcing shell, where it used to be pinned always.
  • pin freezes the curves currently drawn as faint grey ghosts, so the next thing you do can be compared against the last thing you did — lower the dissipation and watch the straight range stretch, or pin at t = 0 and watch the spectrum fill in. Up to four; unpin clears them.
  • clip tail sets where the bottom of the y axis goes. Ticked (the default) it stops just below the dissipation scale: with hyper above 1 the spectrum falls off a cliff there and runs many decades further down, and drawing all of that would squash the interesting part into a few pixels. Untick it to see every scale that was computed — which is what you want at hyper = 1, where the fall-off is gradual, and whenever the question is whether energy is piling up at the smallest scales. A run that is under-resolved shows a bump at the right-hand end, and with the tail clipped that bump is drawn below the axis and looks exactly like a clean run.

anisotropy (3D) — how stretched the eddies are along the background magnetic field, scale by scale. Turbulence in a magnetised plasma does not make round eddies: the smaller they get, the longer and thinner they are along the field. This chart measures that, and it is the one plot where the theory of the last thirty years is directly visible. To see the same thing by eye rather than as a curve, put a display card's scale filter on and slide it up. The first select chooses what goes on the y axis: the ratio k∥/k⊥ (the default, and what everything below describes) or χ, the same measurement in the combination critical balance is actually a statement about.

It is built from the two spectra of the chart above, with no extra work asked of the GPU. For a given amount of energy at small scales, it asks which perpendicular scale and which parallel scale hold that much, and plots one against the other; doing it for a ladder of energies gives the curve. Reading it:

  • The line falls, because eddies get more elongated as they get smaller. Somewhere between −1/2 and −1/3 is the classic critical balance prediction: −1/3 is Goldreich & Sridhar's, where eddies live exactly as long as it takes an Alfvén wave to cross them, and −1/2 is what the same picture gives when flow and field align. The fit control is the spectrum card's, with those two indices and −1 in its box; on pin to field its amplitude is matched to the curve halfway (logarithmically) between the box scale and the dissipation scale, so the line sits through the middle of the measured range rather than at one end of it.
  • There are two curves. The solid one measures the parallel scale along the z axis of the box — kz, in the global mean field; the dashed one measures it along the actual field lines, which are themselves bent, so it is the local mean field. They separate, and the separation is the point: the field lines wander, so beyond a certain scale the z axis stops being the parallel direction at all and the solid curve flattens towards a slope of −1. The dashed curve keeps reporting the real scaling. Measured in the wrong frame, critical balance is invisible — which is why it took a while to find. The dashed curve needs the field-line sampler, so it appears a second after the solid one.
  • The which energy option matches the total or one Elsasser field on its own; an imbalanced run can be anisotropic differently in the two.
  • Two honest caveats. The height of the curve is a convention, not a measurement: it moves if you change Lz, so compare slopes and never absolute values. (That is what the χ setting below is for: it is the combination the convention cancels out of.) And at the left-hand end the parallel scales are whole numbers of box lengths, too coarse to resolve, which flattens the curve there — that is the instrument, not the plasma. Give it a forced run, a while to develop, and read the middle.
  • It is an experimental feature, and the hint says so: expect imperfect agreement at these resolutions. A browser-sized box has perhaps a decade of perpendicular range and a handful of parallel bins under it, which is enough to see the ratio fall and the two curves part company, and not enough to measure an exponent off.

χ = k⊥δb / (k∥vA) — the second setting of that card's first select, and the same measurement said differently. The ratio's height is a convention because stretching the box stretches k∥; but stretching the box also weakens the fluctuation by the same factor, so this combination of the two does not move at all. It is the ratio of the two times in critical balance — how fast an eddy is sheared, over how fast an Alfvén wave crosses it — and the theory's claim is not a slope but a level: χ of order 1, all the way down the cascade. The dashed line at χ = 1 is that claim, and the curve is the measurement.

  • What δb means here. δb² is the energy the field carries above k⊥ — the very quantity the two spectra are matched on, so it costs nothing and is defined at every point of the curve. That is a convention too, at the factor-of-two level, as every δb in this subject is. So read the level as an order of magnitude: χ near 1 is critical balance, χ near 10 or 0.1 would not be, and the third significant figure means nothing.
  • The interesting part is where it is not flat. Half a decade of flat line is a weak plot; what the curve has to say is at its ends. It rises above 1 towards the forcing shell, because the outer scale is driven rather than critically balanced. It rolls off at the dissipation knee, where the energy stops cascading and starts being burnt. And on an imbalanced run the two Elsasser settings separate.
  • Which δb, on E±. What shears a wave packet is the packet coming the other way, so χ+ is built from E+'s own parallel scale and E−'s amplitude, and vice versa. On a balanced run the two settings agree and there is nothing to see; on the imbalanced ones the weaker field turns out to be the strongly sheared one. If one Elsasser field is empty there is nothing to shear the other with, and the card honestly draws nothing.
  • Solid against dashed is not a new measurement. The same δb divides both curves, so they separate by exactly the factor they separate by on the ratio axis — the field-line-wander story above, drawn on the axis where it bears on the physics rather than on a convention.
  • One instrument caveat, measured rather than argued: each field line has to be windowed before its spectrum is taken (its two ends are unrelated), which smears the parallel spectrum over about two bins and returns k∥ slightly high, so χ comes back slightly low. On a synthetic field with a known answer the factor is 0.99, i.e. a percent — far inside the order-of-magnitude reading the level gets anyway.

2D spectrum E(k⊥,k∥) (3D) — the picture the chart above reduces to a curve: how much energy sits at each combination of a perpendicular and a parallel scale, drawn as a heatmap. Bright is more energy (the colorbar under it gives the range). The bright ridge running up and to the right, bending over as it goes, is critical balance. Both axes cover exactly what was measured and no more: across, from the left-hand edge of the first perpendicular band to the right-hand edge of the last (nothing below the first band was ever cut out of the field, so there is no axis for it); up, from the parallel scale the forcing itself injects at — the forcing's z profile is a single cosine over the box, so the first parallel bin is the injection scale — to the last resolved bin. The axis then carries on a little past that last bin, ticks and all, purely so the legend has empty canvas to sit on instead of covering the top left of the picture: the extra strip is real axis with nothing measured in it, and no cell moves against the axis because of it.

  • generate is how it fills in, and it is the only chart here that works that way. Pressing it pauses the run and measures every perpendicular band on that one frozen moment, which takes about a second; every row of the picture then belongs to the same instant instead of to ten different ones. The plot stays as it is afterwards — press Run when you have finished reading it, and press generate again when you want a fresh one. The legend says which moment you are looking at.
  • field line / coordinate is the same snapshot measured in two frames, and switching between them is the lesson. Along the coordinate z axis the ridge goes flat at small perpendicular scales, because the field lines have wandered far enough that z is no longer the parallel direction; measured along the field lines it keeps bending. (Same story as the two curves on the anisotropy chart.) One caveat on the field-line half: the lines of each row are traced through the field of that band rather than through the whole field, so the large-scale wander that does most of the decorrelating is not in them. Measured properly, with the full field's lines, the contrast at small perpendicular scales would be stronger than what is drawn here, not weaker.
  • which energy is the anisotropy card's: the total, or one Elsasser field.
  • log colour / linear colour is the scale the cells are painted on, and the card's hint names whichever one is in force. Log (the default) runs from the same noise floor the spectrum charts use up to the peak, and is the only scale on which an inertial range is visible at all; linear runs from zero to the peak, which shows almost nothing — and that is the point of having it, because it is the honest picture of how much of the energy sits at the outer scale. The colorbar's middle tick follows: the geometric mean of the two ends on the log scale, the arithmetic mean on the linear one.
  • theory slopes (off by default) adds three straight reference lines, legended GS95, B06 and iso on a single line of the legend: k∥ ∝ k⊥2/3 (critical balance, GS95), k∥ ∝ k⊥1/2 (Boldyrev 2006) and k∥ ∝ k⊥ (isotropic, the null case). Each is laid just clear of the upper edge of the filled part of the plot — touching the top edge of the last filled cell where that edge presses hardest against the slope, and above it everywhere else — because in critical balance the relation is the boundary of the excited region rather than the line of brightest cells: the cascade fills everything below k∥(k⊥). So compare the boundary's bend with the three slopes; only the slopes are claims, never the heights, which are set by that rule. “Filled” here means a cell at least twice the noise floor rather than merely at it: a single cell of single-precision fuzz at the top of one column would otherwise drag all three lines up together, by most of the height of the frame.
  • Read the shape, not a number. It is an experimental feature, and the hint says so: at these resolutions there are only about twenty usable parallel bins and the arithmetic is single precision, so the ridge is a fuzzy band rather than a line and agreement with any of the three slopes is imperfect; measuring an exponent off it is a job for a bigger machine. Give it a forced run with some history behind it — in the collision demo, let the packets meet and develop first.

cut trace — a slice through the field along a single line (along y, at the middle of the box). The option picks which pair of components: u_x, u_y · b_x, b_y · |z+|, |z−|. In 3D it has its own z-plane source.

island width (2D, for the tearing demo) — the width W of the magnetic island against time, on a log axis, so the early growth is a straight line and the later slow stage bends over. The legend fits the slope of that straight part and quotes it as a growth rate γ, which is the number to compare against the linear theory value in the preset's note. It goes blank once the curve stops being a straight line, which is not a fault: the rate is genuinely local.

k_y = 2π/L_y mode (2D, for the Kelvin–Helmholtz demo) — the size of the growing mode against time, again on a log axis with a fitted γ. It exists because the energy trace cannot show this at all: the equilibrium shear carries about a million times more energy than the seed, so the total energy sits flat while the instability grows through six decades.

saving a picture or a video

save makes a PNG of the card's current view: the field, the arrows or field lines drawn over it, and the colorbar. The file is named after the page, the quantity and the simulation time, for example taranis-rmhd2d-vorticity-t12.500.png.

rec starts recording that card to a short video and turns into stop. Press it again to stop, or leave it: it stops itself after 30 seconds so a forgotten recording cannot grow without bound. The file is a plain MP4 at 30 frames a second: the page encodes it and assembles it itself, one keyframe every second, which is what makes it play on a phone as well as on a laptop. (Left to record the video for us, the browser writes a fancier kind of MP4 that iPhones and iPads show as a handful of stills.) Where the browser is too old for that, the video is recorded the old way instead and may come out as a WebM. The video is the field canvas alone — arrows, field lines and the colorbar are in the PNG but not in the video. Recording is a browser feature that not every browser has; if yours does not, the button is simply not there, and save still works.

Neither writes anything by itself. When you press stop (or save), a small line appears on the card giving the file's size — and, for a video, how long it is — with a download button and, where the browser can hand a file to another app, a share one; share is what you want on a phone. The picture and the video keep their own lines, so saving a picture does not take a recording away. Nothing is uploaded anywhere: the whole simulation runs on your own machine.

the presets, in one line each

2D

  • forced turbulence — energy fed in continuously at the largest scales; the standard picture of a turbulent cascade, and the one to use for spectra.
  • decaying A / B packets — two letter-shaped blobs, no forcing: watch a very ordered initial condition collapse into the same universal cascade anyway.
  • rmhd variables (φ, ψ) — opens with an empty drawing and asks you to make the initial condition yourself, one click at a time, to see what φ and ψ each do on their own.
  • Kelvin–Helmholtz shear layers — two opposing streams roll up into vortices; raise b0 past U0 and the magnetic field's tension suppresses the whole thing.
  • tearing mode — a current sheet reconnects into a magnetic island, with a growth rate you can measure against linear theory.
  • tearing: X-point collapse — the same sheet with twice the drive, where the island never settles: it collapses the X-point into a second, thinner sheet, which tears in turn. One slider walks it back to the tearing preset above.
  • tearing: island chain — a much larger box seeded at every wavelength at once, so the sheet picks its own: several islands appear, then merge in pairs until one is left.

3D

  • forced turbulence — the same forced cascade with the background field along z, so the parallel and perpendicular spectra can differ.
  • Alfven-wave collision — two wave packets travelling towards each other along the field, meeting head-on in the middle of the box. This is the elementary event that turbulence in a magnetised plasma is built out of. It opens on the sinusoidal pair, whose surfaces fill the box and are the easiest to read, shown as one volume view of the current, where the collision does its work.
the self-test, and what to do when something looks wrong

At the bottom of each app is a Self-test button. It runs a small fixed problem through the same GPU code the live run uses and compares the answers against values computed at higher precision by the original research code, printing a table of relative errors against their tolerances. It is there so you can check for yourself that the browser is solving the same equations.

If a run turns into noise or the numbers go blank, the usual cause is too little dissipation for the resolution: tick auto, or push the diss slider up a little, and press Reset. If the page says it cannot find a GPU, the browser does not have WebGPU enabled.

These apps run in single precision, which is right for looking at things and not a substitute for a research run.

Implementation notes, the numerical contracts and the verification tooling are in the repository, not here.
